Galatea: what matters for visual place recognition on the Moon? An optimal-transport analysis of aggregation and re-ranking

Raunak Sarbajna and Christoph F. Eick · Department of Computer Science, University of Houston · manuscript in preparation

Paper (PDF) Code, benchmark and results Cite

In short

A rover on the Moon has no GPS. It can still locate itself by recognising places: it compares its camera view against a set of reference images with known positions, much as you might recognise a street corner. This is called visual place recognition. It is hard on the Moon for three reasons: the Sun casts black, sharp shadows that move over the lunar day; crater fields look statistically alike everywhere; and lunar soil reflects light in unusual ways.

Benchmark: LunarSim-VPR

Procedural scenes combine a 16.4 km regional DEM (16 m/px) with a 0.3 m/px local DEM, lunar-Lambert photometry with an opposition surge, and cast shadows from both. Each query pose is rendered under seven solar geometries (azimuth 135–315°, elevation 6–60°) against a database fixed at 135°/30°, which separates illumination change from viewpoint change. Test split: 3 unseen scenes, 450 database images, 3,150 queries.

Method: optimal transport in both stages

Stage 1 aggregates DINOv2 ViT-B/14 patch tokens by a semi-relaxed unbalanced OT assignment to 64 clusters and a dust-bin; its relaxation λ ∈ [0, 1] recovers the NetVLAD (λ = 0) and SALAD (λ = 1) assignments. Stage 2 re-ranks the top 10 candidates by partial fused Gromov–Wasserstein matching of SuperPoint keypoints, fused with the global similarity through a saturating rule with bounded override.

Evaluation protocol

Recall@k with a true match defined as < 10 m and < 60° heading difference. All main and assignment-family configurations are trained with three seeds, with frozen and with partially fine-tuned backbones; differences are reported with 95% paired bootstrap confidence intervals over queries. All hyperparameters (fusion β and κ, backbone learning rate) are selected on a held-out validation scene.

The LunarSim-VPR benchmark

No public lunar dataset combines exact camera positions with systematic changes of lighting, so we built a lightweight renderer. Each scene combines a 16.4 km regional elevation model with a detailed 0.3 m local one. Craters follow the size–frequency statistics of real lunar terrain, and distant massifs form the horizon. Light follows the lunar-Lambert reflectance model with an opposition surge, and shadows are cast by both local and regional relief.

The test set has three unseen scenes: 450 map images and 3,150 queries. Every query position is rendered under seven Sun conditions, from the map’s own Sun (C0) to the Sun behind the camera and only 8° above the horizon (C6). Training uses five other scenes lit from many Sun directions.

The method

Galatea is a two-stage system. Both stages are optimal transport problems: they decide how much “mass” to move between two sets of things.

A family of pooling rules

The first stage solves a semi-relaxed unbalanced transport problem with one parameter, λ. At λ = 0 it is exactly the assignment of NetVLAD, at λ = 1 exactly that of SALAD. The dust-bin lets the model throw away features that say nothing about the place, such as black sky.

We prove that the iterations converge at rate λ, that the dust-bin acts as a logistic gate, and that after normalisation the descriptor depends on the transport plan only through a weight per feature. That last result predicts that, with a frozen backbone, the choice of λ should barely matter.

Fusing local and global evidence

The second stage matches keypoints between the query and each candidate, rewarding matches that keep their relative layout. It needs no training and is unaffected by rotating, shifting or scaling either image.

Its score is fused with the global similarity through a saturating rule. We prove that the local evidence this rule needs to overturn the global ranking grows linearly with the global margin, which protects confident global decisions from local matches on moving shadow edges.

Lighting in the training data

Training on the same scenes but with a single Sun direction costs 13.6 points with a frozen backbone and 21.5 points with a fine-tuned one, more than fine-tuning adds to the same model. A more flexible model fits the training light more closely, so fine-tuning only helps together with varied lighting.

The choice of λ has little effect

With a frozen backbone, every λ from 0 to 1 gives the same accuracy within 1.5 points, as the theory predicts. Training cannot choose λ: a trainable λ ends where it starts, and even with a 100-fold learning rate it drifts without converging, so λ should be fixed. NetVLAD’s residual pooling, a different design choice, pulls ahead by 4.5 points.

Fusing local and global evidence

Ranking by local matches alone rescues 15% of queries but breaks 16% that were right. Fused with the global score, it keeps half the rescues and breaks only 2.4%. With fusion, the matchers we tried are within a few points of each other, a smaller effect than fusion itself; the best is CLIPPER (below).

Two more re-ranking tests

CLIPPER, the matcher of LunarLoc

CLIPPER keeps the largest set of keypoint matches whose pairwise distances agree with each other. Added as a fifth scorer, with its tolerance chosen on the validation scene, it is the best fused scorer: 68.7% Recall@1 with MAGSAC++ verification, 2.7 points above partial FGW (95% interval 1.3 to 4.1) and 1.5 above nearest neighbours with MAGSAC++ (0.2 to 2.9).

Exact 3-D distances

Distances between keypoints in the image change when the camera moves, so we gave FGW and CLIPPER the exact 3-D position of every keypoint from the renderer. This did not help. FGW lost 1.8 points (−3.3 to −0.4), and 2.0 to 3.5 points when the query stands 0–8 m from the mapped place; CLIPPER was unchanged. The 3-D distances are almost exact for true matches (0.3% distortion, against 21% in the image), but only about 16% of the keypoints in a query and its true map image have a partner at all, so the structural terms mostly compare keypoints that have nothing to match.

Robustness to camera tilt and viewpoint

The benchmark queries are upright and close to where the map images were taken. To see how far that holds, we changed one property of the query camera at a time (roll, pitch, distance from the mapped place, heading or height), re-rendered all 3,150 test queries at their exact positions under all seven Sun conditions, and searched the same upright map.

Tilted views are rendered exactly: the renderer produces a wider upright view at three times the resolution and reprojects it through the camera rotation, so no image is simply rotated or blurred. Roll turns the camera about its viewing direction, so an upside-down camera sees the same scene upside down.

Roll is the main weakness

A roll of only 1° costs 4 points, 5° costs 19 and 15° costs 40. From 45° on, no model, Earth-trained or lunar-trained, reaches 11%. Fine-tuning the backbone raises every number but loses about as much. No training or map image is ever tilted, and the image features are not rotation-invariant.

Levelling with gravity recovers most of it

Rotating the image back to upright with the rover’s accelerometer brings an upside-down view back to 46.9% (fine-tuned NetVLAD: 72.4%). What remains comes from the black regions outside the tilted frame. With the camera tilted up into the sky, the OT head holds up better than NetVLAD (frozen: 32.1% vs 20.9%), consistent with its dust-bin discarding sky.

Heading and position

Standing 8 m from the mapped place costs 21 points, but turning by 30° costs 42: each place is mapped from one direction, and most of the frame is nearby ground that changes completely when the rover turns. Camera height should also match the map: 1 m or 2.5 m instead of 1.5 m costs 9 to 12 points.

First tests on real Apollo photographs

Everything above comes from simulation. To see what carries over, we built two small benchmarks from the Apollo Hasselblad surface photography, using the panoramas the astronauts shot at each traverse station.

Real photos against real photos

The Apollo Lunar Surface Journal lists which frames form each panorama and where it was taken. From it we kept 79 ordinary surface pans: 1,640 frames at 47 stations on all six landing missions. A query is a frame from one pan; the map is every other pan of the same mission; a match is correct when it comes from the same station. Two pans of one station differ in photographer, time (often a different moonwalk, so a different Sun elevation), exact standpoint and heading. We score Apollo 15, 16 and 17: 958 queries from 41 pans at 19 stations, where a random guess scores 6.5%.

Real photos against a rendered map

This is the setting a rover would face: real queries, and a map rendered from orbital data. We rendered nine Apollo 15 stations, 12 headings each, from the LROC NAC 2 m elevation model of the Hadley–Apennine site, with the Sun placed where it stood when each station's pans were taken. The map views were then made photographic with DESPINA, and compared with plain shaded renders of the same elevation model. 261 real frames from 14 pans are the queries; a random guess scores 11.1%.

Re-ranking real photos

We re-ranked each model’s top 10 on the panoramas with the same keypoint matchers and the fusion settings tuned in LunarSim, without any tuning on real data. Fusion helps every model and every matcher, by 5.5 to 7.8 points, and every interval is above zero; tuning the settings on two missions and testing on the third gives 2.7 to 7.6. The matchers are within about one point of each other. Boulder outlines found with FastSAM, matched the way LunarLoc matches them, changed Recall@1 by −0.9 to +1.4 points.

Simulation gains do not carry over yet

Fine-tuning adds about 12 points in LunarSim, but on real photographs every OT, NetVLAD and SALAD head lands within about 3 points of the Earth-trained model. The result holds with full frames instead of the centre crop, and without queries at the landing site, where the lander itself is visible.

Matching real photos to a rendered map is the hard case

Against the rendered map, the Earth-trained model is at chance. Only the heads fine-tuned on LunarSim clear chance, the first sign that simulation training helps on real imagery. DESPINA's photographic map helps fine-tuned NetVLAD by 7.7 points, but the interval (−0.8 to +14.6) still includes zero.

Which findings survive

Fusion helps on real images as it does in simulation, and GeM pooling is worst on real images too. The real data cannot yet separate OT, NetVLAD and SALAD. These are first, small tests: station positions come from 1971 traverse navigation, many frames show mostly nearby ground, and only 14 pans are scored against the rendered map.

A second simulator: the LunarLoc traverses

LunarLoc (Thomas et al., IEEE Aerospace 2026) released the rover runs behind its paper, rendered in the Lunar Autonomy Challenge simulator. They test our models on terrain and rendering that we did not build, under LunarLoc’s three conditions.

  • Same route, same light. Every model except GeM places 84.7–89.8% of views correctly, and fusion with nearest-neighbour matches adds 7.1–16.4 points.
  • Lighting change. The frozen OT head is highest (58.6%, Earth-trained SALAD 52.4%, with overlapping intervals). Fine-tuned heads fall to 47.1–49.8%, as on Apollo. CLIPPER fusion adds 8.7–13.7 points, nearest neighbours at most 1.9.
  • Driven the other way. No view faces within 30° of a map view, so we count views of the same ground as matches; the frozen OT head is again highest (59.3%).

What this means for lunar navigation

  1. Train under many Sun directions. An illumination sweep in the training data, from a renderer or from elevation-model pipelines, matters more than backbone fine-tuning or the choice of assignment.
  2. Adapt the backbone, then choose the pooling. Fine-tuning adds 11–19 points for every model. Fix λ, since training cannot set it; λ = 0 is the cheapest.
  3. Use local evidence to adjust the global ranking. Local evidence on the Moon is plentiful but unreliable. Fuse it with the global score so that overturning a confident global decision needs strong local evidence. This is the one finding that held on real photographs and in a second simulator; CLIPPER is the strongest matcher we tried, nearest neighbours the cheapest.
  4. Level the image, and map each place from several directions. Rotate images to upright with the rover’s accelerometer before searching, record the map at the camera height used in operation, and include several headings per place. Position errors of a few metres cost little.

These recommendations come from simulation. On real Apollo photographs and in a second simulator the gains from lunar fine-tuning disappear, while fusion keeps helping. Closing the gap between simulation and real photographs, and finding local evidence that survives a Sun change, are the main open problems.

Related work: DESPINA and LunarLoc

DESPINA (Nelson-Archer, Sarbajna and Eick, ISPRS Annals 2026) synthesises photorealistic lunar horizon views for a given location from orbital elevation models with diffusion models. LunarSim gives exact control of the Sun; DESPINA gives realistic appearance for real sites. We used it to render the map for the Apollo 15 test above.

LunarLoc (Thomas et al., IEEE Aerospace 2026) localises a rover against an earlier session by segmenting boulders with FastSAM, placing them in 3-D with stereo and matching them with the CLIPPER consistency graph, with about 1 cm error. Galatea solves a different part of the problem: finding the place from one camera image among stored images. We used LunarLoc’s matcher, its released runs and its boulder detections in the tests above.
